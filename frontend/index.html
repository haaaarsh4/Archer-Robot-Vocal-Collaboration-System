The system

One voice, three machines, and a microphone in the middle

A singer sings. A browser, a Python server and a small family of local models listen, decide, and answer in a synthetic voice. This page is the map: every process, every stage, the contract between them, and the reason each one is built the way it is.

FastAPI on one port44.1 kHz live pathlocal models onlyno cloud dependency

01 The shape of the system

Three ways in, one set of ears

The project is one codebase with three front doors. The live path listens to a microphone and answers in real time. The SingSong path listens to a finished vocal and composes a band around it. The MySong path takes a melody, reads its harmony, and arranges a band from chords. All three share the same pitch, rhythm, key and audio code, which is why a fix in one makes the others better.

02 The live path

What happens between one note and the next

The live path is a chain of small, replaceable stages. Each stage has one job and one output shape, and the next stage never reaches back into the previous one. That is what makes it possible to swap YIN for RMVPE, or a DSP voice for a neural one, without rewriting the performance.

03 Frame budget

Twenty-three milliseconds is the whole world

At 44,100 Hz a 1024-sample frame is 23.2 ms of audio. Every real-time stage has to finish well inside that window, or the next frame is already waiting. The design keeps the expensive parts off the critical path instead of hoping they get faster.

On the hot path

Cheap, bounded, per frame

YIN on two 512-sample hops, one MFCC pass, a few deque updates, a key lookup, and a mode decision. No disk, no network, no model loading, no allocation of a new graph.

Off the hot path

Expensive, cached, or separate

RMVPE runs on a rolling window every few hops. Neural voice conversion lives in its own process. SingSong and MySong render an uploaded file after the fact, where a two-second wait is acceptable because nobody is singing into it.

04 Runtime

A small family of processes, and why they are not one

The app is deliberately not a monolith at runtime, even though it is one repository. Each boundary below exists because something inside it is slow, optional, or likely to fail in a way that must not take the performance down with it.

06 Decisions

Seven choices that shaped everything after them

Architecture is mostly a record of what you refused to do. These are the decisions that still constrain the code today, written as decision, reason and consequence rather than as a feature list.

D1 · Local first

No note leaves the machine

Every model runs on the same computer as the microphone. The consequence is real: no API keys, no per-note cost, no network jitter in the audio path. It also means the project ships code, not a hosted service, and the person running it owns the data.

D2 · Separate processes

Slow work cannot stall the loop

RVC and the language model are sidecars. If the voice model takes 2.5 seconds to convert a note, the DSP voice still answers on time; if Ollama is not running, the chat reports it and the stage keeps singing. The boundary is a timeout, not a promise.

D3 · Two pitch engines

YIN for speed, RMVPE for truth

YIN is the default because it fits the frame budget on a CPU. RMVPE is available when accuracy matters more than latency, with a rolling window and a cached result between hops. One interface, two behaviours, chosen live.

D4 · Pre-rendered vocables

The bank is the fast path

Neural conversion is expensive; a note is not. The bank is rendered once, offline, then pitch-shifted and cached by semitone at runtime. The singer hears a neural timbre without waiting for a neural network on every note.

D5 · Per-stem conversion

One pitch per call

A choir mixed down to one signal gives the voice model several simultaneous pitches and a smeared result. Converting each detuned stem separately keeps every call monophonic, and lets harmony stems rotate across voices instead of cloning one.

D6 · Discrete tokens

SingSong predicts codes, not samples

Waveform generation at 16 kHz for ten seconds is a very long sequence. The paper's factorization into semantic, coarse and fine tokens makes the problem tractable, and each stage can be trained and evaluated on its own.

D7 · Exact decoding

MySong uses Viterbi, not sampling

A chord sequence is a path through a small state space, so exact inference is possible and reproducible. The semi-Markov decoder can change chord on any beat, which fixed one-chord-per-bar grids could not express.

Harmony

Ten ways to answer without becoming the lead

A fixed third above the melody is one idea, repeated forever. This engine has ten musical functions instead, and a public rule for choosing between them. The first rule is not musical at all: Archer decides whether the robot may sing.

10 modessovereignty checked firstkey re-estimated once a secondexact, ordered selection

01 The premise

A chord is one option, not the assumption

Traditional harmonisers assume the answer to “what should the second voice sing?” is a pitch a fixed interval away. That works for a hymn. It does not work for a solo voice carrying a phrase, because a second voice that doubles every note at a third is no longer accompanying; it is competing for the same musical role.

The modes here are best read as functions, not intervals. Unison shadowing reinforces identity. Drone support holds a floor. Call and response gives the singer space and then answers into it. Timbral thickening changes colour without changing pitch. Each one makes a different claim on the listener's attention, and the engine's job is to pick the claim that fits the moment.

The design constraint that matters most: the robot must be able to stop. Every musical decision sits behind a sovereignty switch that is checked before the key, the tempo, the phrase, or the singer's pitch. A harmony engine that can be talked out of silence by a fast tempo is not a collaborator.

02 The vocabulary

The ten functions, one by one

Five of these are chosen automatically, three are manual or configured defaults, one is fusion-only, and one is the stop. The distinction is not cosmetic: it is the difference between a behaviour the engine can infer from context and a behaviour a person has to ask for.

Automatic · default

Unison shadowing

Sings the singer's exact pitch in another voice. No interval, no scale snap. The identity is reinforcement: the same note, wider. It is the default because it is the least presumptuous thing a second voice can do.

Automatic · long note

Timbral thickening

Holds the same pitch but changes the vowel, brightness and attack, so the double becomes a colour rather than a copy. Triggered after a note has been sustained for thirty frames, where a second onset would sound like an error and a pitch change would sound like harmony the singer did not ask for.

Automatic · fast

Contour following

While the melody holds still, it sits in unison. When the pitch genuinely moves, it glides a step above a rising line or below a falling one, then settles back when the motion stops. Hysteresis keeps it from flickering at the threshold; a continuous timbre lean tracks the shape even when the pitch does not move.

Automatic · silence

Drone support

One note, one vowel, one breath per phrase, locked near the tonic and held under the line. It is not “the melody an octave down”; it waits for a phrase to begin, opens once, and stops when the phrase stops. The pitch is clamped into a singable low register so it stays a voice rather than a foghorn.

Automatic · phrase end

Call and response

When the singer stops, the answer begins: a phrase with the same rhythm as the call, arriving on the tonic by step. The live responder composes it from the last phrase; the offline renderer composes a better one from the whole take, because it can see the ending before it plays it.

Manual

Octave reinforcement

Doubles the line an octave down, with the three things a singer actually does on a low note: a brighter vowel, a narrower vowel, and less level. Without those, an octave double reads as the same voice gone muddy rather than a second singer joining underneath.

Manual

Delayed response

Sings the pitch the singer used one beat ago. The decision supplies the note; a separate echo effect supplies the repeating, decaying taps. Keeping those apart is deliberate: a delay pedal repeats whatever came out of it, and does not need to know the note.

Manual

Solo humming

One closed-mouth voice, barely there. It is the quietest possible presence: not a harmony, not a double, just another body in the room. Useful when the arrangement needs company without a musical statement.

Fusion only

Triadic harmony

A conventional chord tone above the melody, unlocked only when fusion mode is explicitly on. It is the one mode that behaves like a traditional harmoniser, and it is opt-in because that vocabulary belongs to a different musical context.

Override

Protocol silence

The stop. It returns no pitch, no note and no sound, and it is checked before every other branch. If sovereignty is off, or the current phoneme belongs to the protocol-sensitive set, the answer is silence even if a mode button is lit.

04 Key and scale

The robot never sings outside the key it has inferred

Modes that invent a pitch (contour following, the fusion chord) are rounded to the nearest degree of the inferred key before anything is sung. Modes that return the singer's own note are deliberately exempt: rounding a unison is how a double ends up a few cents beside the singer instead of with them.

The key is inferred once a second from the last 256 pitched frames. The distribution of pitch classes is correlated against Krumhansl–Kessler major and minor profiles, and the best-fitting root and mode become the current key. The drone is anchored to that root, not to whatever note just passed; the call-and-response answer is built from its scale degrees, not rounded into them afterwards.

Scale lock is therefore a small, explicit exception list, not a blanket rule. It touches contour following and triadic harmony. It does not touch unison, octave, delay, timbral thickening or drone. The reason is simple: those five already have a trustworthy pitch, and the two that do not are the two that calculate.

05 Voice leading

Movement is the difference between a chord and a choir

When a mode needs more than one note, the voicing search prefers the inversion that moves each voice the shortest distance from where it already was. That single constraint is what makes a sequence of chords sound like voices holding a conversation instead of a keyboard being pressed.

The test suite generates 120 progressions, voices each chord with the project's search and with a naive root-position baseline, and measures the total semitone movement of the upper voices plus the number of parallel perfect fifths and octaves. The search cuts mean movement from 1.56 to 0.90 semitones per transition and parallel perfects from 3.89 to 3.61 per hundred transitions, while keeping a median span of nine semitones. The point is not the decimal; it is that the improvement is measured on the same progressions the baseline sees.

06 Sovereignty

The switch is not a setting. It is the first rule.

The protocol guard is checked before the key, before the tempo, before the phrase, and before any mode's own logic. When it is off, the engine returns silence with a reason, not a quiet note. When the current phoneme is in the protected class list, the same thing happens even with a mode button lit.

This is a political requirement expressed as control flow. The robot is a guest in Archer's performance. A guest who can only be asked to leave after the harmony has already been chosen is not a guest; it is a collaborator with veto power over silence. Putting the check first makes silence the default state and sound the exception that has to be earned.

Try it in the demo below. Turn sovereignty off, pick any mode, and sing. The mode stays selected, the meters still move, and the robot stays silent, because the check runs before the mode does.

Vocable synthesis

A voice built from vowels, not words

The robot does not speak Cree. It sings vocables: open vowels shaped like the ones Archer uses, pitched to the note the harmony engine chose, and optionally re-skinned through a synthetic voice model. This page follows that sound from a bank of recordings to the speaker.

three enginesformant shapingsolo · duet · choirneural conversion, per stem

01 The material

Why a vowel, and not a word

A vocable is a sung syllable that carries no dictionary meaning: aah, ohh, mmm. It is the oldest thing a voice can do with a melody, and it is the right unit here for two reasons.

It stays out of the language's way. Archer's practice is grounded in Cree singing, and the project includes real Cree translation and sentiment tools elsewhere. The robot's voice is not one of them. Making it sing recognizable Cree words would put a synthetic speaker inside a language conversation it has no standing in. A vocable can echo the resonance of Cree vowels without claiming to pronounce them.

It is the smallest unit that can be shaped musically. A vowel has a pitch, a loudness, an attack and a spectral envelope, and every one of those can be controlled independently. A word brings consonants, timing and meaning, and every one of those would have to be modelled before a single note could be sung. The vocable keeps the problem at the size of the voice.

02 Three engines

The same decision, rendered three ways

The harmony engine emits a note, a vowel, a texture and an action. What turns that into samples is a separate choice, set once in config.yaml. The three engines trade realism against latency, and the default is the one that keeps a live performance live.

03 The bank

Pay for the neural network once, not on every note

A live neural conversion takes about two and a half seconds on this machine. A note lasts a fraction of that. Running the model per note would make the robot late for every phrase, so the bank inverts the problem: convert a small set of takes ahead of time, then shift them at runtime.

04 The vowel

Formants are the shape of the mouth

A vowel is not a frequency. It is a pattern of resonances: peaks in the spectrum that the throat and mouth impose on whatever the vocal folds are doing. The first two peaks, F1 and F2, do most of the work. Move them and aah becomes ee without changing the pitch at all.

The synthesizer treats the vowel as a small set of targets, not a recording. F1 and F2 are blended toward the active phoneme's values, a high shelf adds brightness, and a notch-and-peak pair shapes nasality. The singer's own MFCCs and spectral centroid drive those targets in heuristic mode, with an influence of 0.6, so the robot's mouth follows the singer's mouth rather than sitting at a neutral midpoint.

05 The ensemble

Several voices, not one voice turned up

A choir preset is not a louder solo. It is several independently rendered layers, each with its own small detune, onset jitter and vocal-tract scale, mixed together and sent through a short algorithmic reverb. The differences are what the ear reads as separate people.

The texture is chosen per mode. Unison defaults to choir, octave to duet, delayed response and call-and-response to solo, contour and drone to duet. The parameters live in synthesis.texture: voice count, detune spread, formant spread, jitter, reverb and level. A thirty-millisecond crossfade removes clicks at note boundaries, and the reverb is built from a handful of comb and allpass filters rather than an impulse response, so it costs almost nothing and never needs a file.

The detune is deliberately not random per note. It is locked per phrase, so a voice keeps its own slightly sharp or flat identity instead of wandering between notes. That is the difference between a section and a chorus effect.

06 The small physics

What makes a held note sound held

A synthesised note is not one oscillator. It is an envelope, a slow pitch movement, a little breath, a filter that follows the mouth, and a join to the note before it. Each of those is a small parameter, and each one is audible when it is wrong.

The phoneme profile is measured, not guessed. Thirteen MFCCs and a spectral centroid are extracted from each voiced frame, then mapped to three values: brightness from 0 to 1, vowel colour from front to back, and nasality from oral to fully nasal. With a trained classifier the mapping is learned; without one, the heuristic rules in analysis/phonetic_analysis.py do the same job. The synthesizer only ever sees the three numbers, so the same profile can drive a formant synth, a bank or a neural voice without changing the analysis code.

07 The neural voice

A modelled timbre, one stem at a time

RVC is a voice conversion model: it takes audio and a target voice model, and returns the same performance in that model's timbre. Here it runs in a separate process on port 8801, so a two-and-a-half-second conversion can never block the audio loop. The configured models are separate trained voices, not recordings of Archer.

The important detail is that the converter receives stems, not the mix. A solo voice is one stem. A duet is two. A choir is several, each slightly detuned. If the ensemble were mixed first, the model would hear several pitches at once and produce a smeared, unstable conversion. Keeping the stems separate means every call is monophonic, which is the only condition the model was trained for.

The lead always converts through the voice model the performer chose. Harmony stems rotate across the other configured voices, so a neural choir sounds like a room of different people rather than one person cloned at several pitches. The sidecar processes one conversion at a time, so a live take caps how many ensemble voices are converted; an offline render can afford to convert every one.

08 What to listen for

Three things the ear checks first

Attack. Does the note begin with a consonant-like click, or with breath? The 30 ms crossfade and the per-voice jitter exist for this. Vowel. Does the double sound like a different mouth, or the same mouth doubled? Formant spread is the control. Blend. Can you hear the lead above the ensemble without the ensemble disappearing? That is the level and reverb balance, and it is the difference between a duet and a wall.

Cree sentiment

What the words carry, under the melody

Three layers sit between a sung phrase and a robot that seems to understand it. The phrase becomes text, the text becomes English, and the English becomes an emotional reading. Each layer is separate, each one can be checked on its own, and none of them pretends to be the others.

nêhiyawêwin · Plains CreeSRO + syllabicsfrom-scratch translationvalence + arousal

01 The chain

Three steps, and why they are not one model

It would be convenient to hand a microphone to one large multimodal model and ask for “the emotion of this Cree sentence.” It would also make every mistake invisible. Splitting the chain means a transcription error, a translation error and a sentiment error each have a different address.

02 Transcription

Whisper was never going to transcribe Cree

Whisper's language="cr" is not a Plains Cree decoder. It selects the English decoder and attaches a Cree language tag, and what comes back for sung Cree is English words that sound vaguely like the vowels. The project measured that, stopped using it for Cree, and built a separate path.

The Cree path uses Meta's MMS-1b-all with both crk adapters, a lexicon and a character model built from this project's own Cree texts, and a CTC beam search constrained so it cannot emit an English word. Before recognition, Demucs can isolate the vocal from the accompaniment, and a harmonic vocal hint keeps the sung pitch content from being discarded by the separator.

English speech still goes through faster-whisper, because for English it is excellent. The demo below lets you choose which language is being spoken. The transcript is shown as it arrives, followed by the checked version, so a rough guess never gets mistaken for a final reading.

Silence is handled explicitly. A span with no voiced frames is not sent to the recogniser at all; it is labelled as silence, which is both faster and more honest than asking a language model to transcribe a breath.

03 Orthography

One language, three ways to write it

Plains Cree is written in Roman orthography (SRO), in Canadian Aboriginal Syllabics, and in the anglicised respelling used by learners and singers who did not grow up with either. The app has to move between all three without losing a long vowel or a w.

SRO uses digraphs and diacritics: ê for a long e, â, î, ô, and combinations such as tw or kw that are one sound, not two. Syllabics writes a consonant and its vowel as a single glyph, with a separate final form for a consonant that closes a syllable. A naive character-by-character mapping fails on both.

The converter works from a syllabary table, not from a transliteration service. It normalises macrons to circumflexes, folds optional doubled vowels, syllabifies before mapping, and round-trips every word in the project's corpus back to the exact SRO it started from. That round trip is the test: 12,372 words, zero failures, zero syllable-count failures.

04 Translation

Building a translator where almost no parallel text exists

There is no off-the-shelf Plains Cree–English model to fine-tune. The translation layer is a transformer encoder–decoder trained from scratch on the material that could be assembled and permissioned: community-contributed text from the Cree corpus, aligned sentences, and vocabulary from Watkins' 1865 dictionary.

Cree is polysynthetic, so a single word can carry what English needs a clause to say. Tokenisation is therefore the first engineering problem, not an afterthought. The project uses a Cree-aware segmentation step (Morfessor and a finite-state analyser where available) before a shared SentencePiece vocabulary, so the model sees morphemes rather than an arbitrary split of a long word.

At inference, the model decodes with beam search, keeping several candidate sentences alive at each step and returning the highest-scoring complete one. The model is deliberately small, because a large model on a small corpus memorises the corpus rather than learning to generalise.

05 Sentiment

Two axes, because “happy” is not one number

Valence says how positive or negative a line is. Arousal says how calm or intense it is. A quiet lament and a furious one are both negative, and they should not produce the same robot. The app reads both axes, from different evidence, and combines them into one of three responses.

Valence · from words

RoBERTa first, VADER as the fallback

The primary model is a pretrained RoBERTa sentiment classifier, mapped from its classes to a −1 to +1 score. VADER's compound score is the second backend, and the response always reports which one ran. Valence is never inferred from pitch: loudness and register cannot tell joy from grief without a harmonic reference, so the app does not pretend they can.

Arousal · words + voice

Half text, half delivery

Text arousal combines the model's emotional charge with intensifiers, exclamation marks, capitals and sentence length. Audio arousal combines pitch range, mean loudness and dynamic variance measured from the take. When a recording is present the two are averaged 50/50, so the same sentence whispered and shouted moves.

01 Why retrieval

A confident guess is the failure mode

Ask a general language model about a song it has never heard of and it will rarely say “I do not know.” It will produce a plausible answer in a confident tone. For an assistant speaking about a living artist's work, that is not a minor inaccuracy; it is fabrication with a friendly voice.

Retrieval-augmented generation changes the order of operations. The question is first used to search Archer's notes. Only the passages that search actually returns are placed in the prompt, under a header that says what they are. The system prompt instructs the model to answer from those passages, and to say plainly when they do not contain the answer. The model is still capable of ignoring that instruction, which is why the sources come back with every reply: a reader can see what the answer was built from.

The boundary is the feature. The assistant can discuss a song's backstory in depth because the notes contain it. It cannot invent a memory Archer never wrote down, and it is told not to try.

02 Indexing

Turning notes into something searchable

Every .md and .txt file in song_notes/ is read, split into chunks of roughly 900 characters along paragraph boundaries, and embedded into a 384-dimensional vector by all-MiniLM-L6-v2 on the CPU.

The chunk size is a compromise between two failures. Too large and a passage about the wrong song dilutes the one relevant paragraph; too small and a thought gets cut before it reaches its point. Paragraph-aware splitting keeps whole thoughts together. A paragraph that is longer than one chunk is windowed instead, and the 150-character overlap covers the seam.

Rebuilding every embedding on every start would be wasteful, so the index is incremental. Each file's content is hashed, and the hash is stored in a manifest alongside the vector offsets. On the next start, an unchanged file reuses its cached vectors; only a changed file is re-chunked and re-embedded. Changing one note does not re-read the corpus.

03 Retrieval

Four passages, and a floor below which nothing is used

The question is embedded with the same model and compared against every stored chunk by cosine similarity. The four closest chunks are returned, but only those above a minimum score of 0.15. A weak match is not included to fill the quota.

Both sides of the comparison are normalised, so the dot product is the cosine. The search is exact, not approximate: there is no vector database and no index structure beyond a single matrix multiply. At the size of a song-notes folder that is faster than maintaining an approximate index, and it cannot miss a passage because of a hash collision.

The same query returns the source filename for each chunk. Those filenames travel back to the browser with the reply, so the assistant's answer is always accompanied by the notes it came from. This is not a citation bolted on afterwards; it is the retrieval result itself.

05 The model

Two ways to run a model on your own machine

The backend is a configuration choice, not a rewrite. Ollama runs as a local server and manages models by name; llama.cpp loads a GGUF file inside the Python process. Both keep every token on the machine.

Default

Ollama

Listens on 127.0.0.1:11434. The default model is an 8-billion-parameter instruction model, quantised to run on a laptop. Pull it once, then the app only sends HTTP to localhost. If the local server is not running yet, the endpoint returns a clear message instead of a connection traceback.

Alternative

llama.cpp

Loads a .gguf file in-process. One less service to start, at the cost of the app holding the model's memory. Useful on a machine where installing a background server is inconvenient, or where the model file is already managed by hand.

Generation is deliberately conservative: temperature 0.4, at most 500 new tokens. A grounded answer should sound like a knowledgeable person speaking briefly, not like a model performing fluency. The cap also keeps a reply from turning into an essay that outruns the four passages it was given.

Both models run through this same choice. The general model answers the project and song questions, and the Cree model is a second Ollama tag beside it. Everything about that second model starts in section 08.

09 The base

A general model, taught to read a different language

A model trained from nothing on a million words has to learn the language and the task at the same time. This one starts from Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, which already reads and follows instructions, and adds a small set of weights and a Cree vocabulary.

The added weights are LoRA adapters: low-rank matrices attached to the attention and feed-forward projections, trained while the base weights stay exactly as they were. Rank 64 on an A100, rank 32 on a 16 GB card. There is no second copy of the model, and the adapter file is a few hundred megabytes instead of fifteen gigabytes, which is why a run can be stopped, moved and resumed like an ordinary file.

The vocabulary is the part that matters most for a language the base model has barely seen. The base tokenizer spends over four tokens on a Cree word, splitting it into pieces that were never meant to be read together. A SentencePiece model trained on the Cree corpus, filtered and then grafted onto the existing vocabulary, spends about a third fewer tokens on the same text, and the run records the measured figure in its tokenizer audit. The full profile adds about four thousand new pieces.

A new token cannot start at random. It starts at the average of the base model's embeddings for the pieces it replaces, so a word that was spelled n · ê · hi · yaw · ê · win already means something on its first day. Random rows have to be learned from scratch, and with a corpus this size they never fully are. The table is then frozen. The input and output embedding matrices together hold about 1.1 billion parameters, and in fp32 with gradients and Adam state that is roughly 11 GB, the difference between fitting a 16 GB card and dying on the first step.

11 Cleaning

What is kept out is part of the design

More text is not automatically more language. A corpus assembled from the open web arrives with English sentences, OCR noise, the same lesson in four places and a scan whose syllabics never decoded.

Text is split into sentences and checked one at a time. A sentence has to look like Cree in its function words and its character inventory, and the share of words that are recognisably Cree has to clear a floor. A PDF page that is mostly English with a Cree title is dropped, and a bag-of-words file is allowed to contribute vocabulary counts without ever being read as prose.

Duplicates are removed twice. An exact match is easy; a near-duplicate check catches the same sentence with a different line break, a stray hyphen or one changed ending, which is the form most copied lessons arrive in. The split is made by whole document, not by sentence, so a story cannot appear in training and again in the test set.

After the split there is a second leakage pass. Anything in dev or test that is a near-duplicate of a training sentence is moved into training. A benchmark scored on the sibling of a sentence the model memorised is not a held-out score, and a flattering number from that setup is worse than no number at all.

12 Training

Three passes, each with one job

Continued pretraining teaches the language. Instruction tuning teaches the shape of the answers. Preference tuning teaches it to prefer the faithful answer over the fluent one.

Continued pretraining reads the cleaned Cree text as packed 2,048-token windows, with no padding wasted between documents. Ten percent of the blocks are English, drawn from a held-out Wikipedia slice, and that replay is the reason the model still follows an English instruction after a week of Cree. Three passes at a learning rate of 2e-4.

Instruction tuning uses the chat template and scores only the assistant's reply. The question is context, not something to reproduce. The examples cover translation in both directions, SRO and syllabics, grounded answers from the song notes, and questions the notes cannot answer, where the correct reply is to say so. Two passes at 1.5e-4, deliberately lower, because over-training this stage flattens every reply into the same shape.

Preference tuning builds its pairs from the training translations. The gold sentence is the chosen answer; the rejected one has a dropped final clause, a flattened long vowel, an English word pushed in, a changed number, or its word order reversed. Those are the failures a fluent model actually makes. The objective is DPO with a beta of 0.1, and the reference is the same model with its adapters switched off, so no second copy is loaded.

Each stage checkpoints every ten minutes and resumes from the newest checkpoint that has both its trainer state and an intact adapter header. The stage's root folder is written only after training returns, so an interrupted stage never looks finished. That is what makes a dropped SSH session a pause rather than a restart.

13 Measurement

Scored against its own base, not against a leaderboard

The benchmark runs the same held-out sentences through the base model with the adapters off and the tuned model with them on. The only thing that changes is the training, so the comparison is paired.

Translation is scored with chrF++ and BLEU, but also with three measures that matter more here. Validity asks whether every generated word exists in the lexicon or obeys Cree phonotactics, because an invented word is the failure a speaker notices first. Code-switch counts English leaking into a Cree answer. Syllabics checks whether the script is actually written, not just named.

The benchmark also measures the things a translation score cannot see. Does the same question get the same answer twice under greedy decoding? Does it still follow an English instruction after the Cree training? Does it decline a question the notes cannot answer, and does it also answer the ones they can? What does a Cree word cost in tokens now? Does its own confidence rise when its answer is better? Each of those is a separate probe with a fixed input list, so two runs of the same adapter produce the same numbers.

14 Running it

One file, one command, resumable

The whole pipeline is a single Python file. It downloads the corpus, cleans it, builds the tokenizer, trains in three stages, benchmarks the result and exports a GGUF. Nothing in it needs Colab, Drive or a notebook.

On a 40 GB card the 7B recipe takes roughly two to four hours plus the corpus download. A 16 GB card uses a shorter context and a smaller adapter and takes about twice as long. A 14B run on 80 GB takes six to ten hours, and a 32B run about a day, with the merge needing a machine with enough RAM to hold two copies of the model.

If the process stops, the same command continues it. Every finished stage is recorded against a fingerprint of its inputs and is skipped, and an interrupted training stage resumes from its newest intact checkpoint with the optimizer and the learning rate schedule. Changing the base model, the context length, the LoRA rank or the corpus invalidates exactly the stages that depended on it, so a new run cannot inherit numbers from an old one.

The export merges the adapter into a full precision copy, converts it with llama.cpp, quantises it to Q4_K_M and writes a Modelfile beside it. That file is what makes the model a single Ollama tag on the machine that runs the app.

SingSong takes a finished vocal and composes an instrumental around it. Not a backing track chosen from a library: a sequence generated to follow the performance. This is a paper-faithful implementation of Google Research and IRCAM's 2023 system, built from the token model up.

01 The idea

Predict codes, not waveforms

Generating ten seconds of 16 kHz audio sample by sample is a sequence of 160,000 decisions. Predicting a structured code sequence for the same ten seconds is a sequence of a few thousand. SingSong's central move is to make the second problem stand in for the first.

A neural audio codec turns the instrumental into discrete tokens at three time scales. A semantic encoder describes what the vocal is doing at 25 tokens per second. A coarse codec describes the body of the instrumental at 200 codes per second. A fine codec adds the top end at 400 codes per second. The model learns the conditional distribution of those codes given the vocal, and the codec turns the result back into sound.

The factorization is the architecture:

P(Sem(y), Coarse(y) | Feats(x)) = P(Fine(y) | Coarse(y)) · P(Coarse(y) | Sem(y), Feats(x)) · P(Sem(y) | Feats(x))

The first two factors are one T5-style encoder–decoder. It reads 250 semantic tokens of the vocal for a ten-second clip and emits 2,250 tokens: 250 semantic tokens for the instrumental, then 2,000 coarse tokens. The third factor is a decoder-only transformer that reads the 2,000 coarse tokens as a prefix and emits 4,000 fine tokens. Each stage can be trained, evaluated and sampled on its own.

02 The token layout

Three rates, three vocabularies, one sequence

The vocabularies are not shared. Semantic, coarse and fine tokens each have their own range, and each position in the output has a mask that makes every token outside its range impossible. That is how one decoder can emit a structured sequence without a separate head for every stream.

At 16 kHz, ten seconds is 160,000 samples. The factorized sequence is 6,250 tokens for the same span, and it is the sequence the model is trained to predict.

03 The model

T5 for the structure, a decoder for the detail

Stage 1+2 is a T5-style encoder–decoder trained with teacher forcing and cross-entropy on every target code. It reads the vocal's semantic tokens and writes the instrumental's semantic and coarse tokens. Stage 3 is a decoder-only transformer that reads the coarse prefix and writes the fine tokens.

Both stages use fixed sinusoidal positional encodings rather than T5's relative bias, pre-RMSNorm, gated GEGLU feed-forwards and tied output embeddings. Dropout is 0.1. Sampling uses a temperature of 0.85 with no top-k by default, and every invalid vocabulary subset is masked to negative infinity at each position. The base shape follows t5.1.1.base; the code supports the XL shape, though the published 3B run is a cluster job, not a laptop job.

The default long-form scheduler generates each ten-second window independently and crossfades with a complementary 50% overlap. The paper describes prompting the decoder with the previous five seconds; that path is implemented as an option for a checkpoint trained with the continuation objective, but it is out of distribution for a model trained on ten-second block layouts. The code states the choice rather than inventing a prompt and hoping.

04 The data

What it takes to teach a model to arrange

The published system used a very large private corpus. This implementation trains on what can be downloaded and licensed: MUSDB18-HQ for the general model, and a small, attributed Cree singing corpus for the adaptation.

Training pairs are vocal and instrumental stems, not mixtures. The pipeline accepts a stem layout directly, or a folder of mixtures with prepare --separate, which runs Demucs two-stem separation and refuses to train on a mixture as if it were an instrumental. Clips are filtered before tokenisation: an instrumental whose peak RMS is below −25 dBFS is dropped, and so is a pair whose vocal is 5 dB louder than its instrumental.

The paper's most important data choice is also implemented: white noise at σ = 0.01 is added to the vocal before feature extraction, at training and inference, and the vocal is represented only by semantic codes, never by its coarse codes. Separation artifacts in a “vocal” stem can otherwise teach the model to reconstruct the instrumental from the artifacts and then fail on a clean vocal. The noise and the semantic-only conditioning are the paper's fix, and this implementation follows it.

MySong

Chords that follow the melody, not a grid

MySong reads a vocal melody, infers its key, chooses a chord sequence, and arranges a band around it. It began as a reimplementation of Microsoft's 2008 MySong and became a different decoder: flexible harmonic rhythm, a learned chord classifier, an exact semi-Markov search, and a band that knows more than one tradition.

01 The problem

A melody does not come with its harmony

Given only a tune, there are usually many chord sequences that fit. A good accompaniment is not the one with the most correct labels; it is the one that supports the phrase, changes when the phrase changes, and leaves room for the voice. That is a sequence problem, not a classification problem.

The 2008 paper framed it as a hidden Markov model: one node per measure, sixty chord states, a transition matrix learned from lead sheets, and a melody observation table. Viterbi finds the most likely path. It is elegant, fast, and it assumes one chord per bar, which is exactly the assumption most real songs break.

This implementation keeps the probabilistic framing and removes the grid. A chord can last 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12 or 16 quarter notes, with a duration prior learned per meter and a separate prior for where in the bar changes happen. The decoder is a semi-Markov HMM, so a chord can begin on any beat. A truncated final chord is allowed, which means a 67-beat take does not have to pretend it is 68.

02 What changed

Six changes from the paper, each with a reason

01 · Harmonic rhythm

Chords can change on any beat

A fixed one-chord-per-bar grid cannot express a two-chord bar or a chord held for four bars. The semi-Markov decoder scores spans of eight possible durations, with priors learned per meter. The paper's fixed grid is still available as an evaluation baseline.

02 · Observation model

A classifier, not a duration table

The paper's P(note | chord) table ignores note order and octave. This implementation fits a multinomial logistic regression on 52 features: metric chroma, downbeat chroma, onset chroma, tail chroma, log duration, bar phase and mode. It is exported to numpy, so inference needs no scikit-learn.

03 · Key

A learned 24-way classifier

Krumhansl–Schmuckler correlation gets 55% exact key accuracy on held-out OpenEWLD, mostly confusing a key with its relative major or minor. A linear classifier trained on the corpus's key labels, transposition-augmented across all 24 keys, reaches 93.2% on the test split. It is a 24 × 12 matrix, not a model file.

04 · Context

A BiGRU tagger sees the phrase

A span classifier cannot see what came before it. A two-layer bidirectional GRU over per-beat features emits 60 chord log-probabilities per beat. It is trained with label smoothing and early stopping on a validation split carved out of the training songs. When its weights are present it is the observation model; otherwise the numpy classifier runs.

05 · Inference

Exact paths, real posteriors

Viterbi over the semi-Markov model is exact. Forward–backward gives a posterior over chords for every span, so the panel can show alternatives and confidence. The posterior is tempered per beat before display because tagger errors are correlated; without that, every long chord reads as 100%. The mean chosen confidence is 0.62 against 0.53 core accuracy.

06 · Sound

Sampled instruments, arranged

The first renderer was additive numpy: clean and plainly an oscillator. The default now renders each stem through FluidSynth and a General MIDI SoundFont, then applies balance, stereo width, convolution room, bus compression and a limiter. The numpy synth remains the deterministic fallback, and a MIDI file is always written.

A Cree or Métis song should not have to sound like a pop demo before it can sound like itself. The band presets lead with the forms this project is for, and the Western styles sit after them as additional choices, not as the default frame.

04 Training

Five hundred songs to prove it, four thousand to hear it

The base corpus is OpenEWLD, the public-domain subset of the Essen folksong collection: 502 songs, 52,417 melody notes, 19,569 chord spans. Splits are by song, 409 train, 49 dev, 44 test. The model is deterministic given a seed.

Five hundred lead sheets are enough to prove a decoder; making the observer strong takes more, which is why the large corpus exists. It is built from the 5,204-score AutoHarmonizer/Wikifonia archive, with two kinds of duplicate removed: normalised title and, more importantly, a transposition-invariant melody signature. Title matching alone missed 40 of the 44 test songs; the signature check removes them. The result is 4,571 extra scores with zero melody overlap against OpenEWLD train, dev or test.

The large tagger is trained by streaming one score at a time and keeping only the per-beat features. A naive load of five thousand songs plus the training graph exceeded 10 GB on a 27 GB laptop; the streaming path stays around 1.5 GB. The structural tables still come from OpenEWLD, which isolates the effect of the extra data and keeps the evaluation honest.

01 Method

What counts as evidence here

A single hand-picked example can make any system look good. The harness therefore prefers many randomised cases with known ground truth, and it records the sample size, the distribution and the failures, not just the mean.

Three kinds of test are used. Synthetic ground truth generates signals where the correct answer is known exactly: a note at 220 Hz, a pattern at 96 BPM, a chord progression with a written key. 02 Pitch

Cents, not “sounds about right”

236 synthesised tones sweep the singing range with vibrato, breath noise and level changes. The detector's answer is compared to the frequency it was given, in cents, and the error distribution is plotted rather than averaged into a single number.

03 Notes and rhythm

Did it hear the note, and did it hear when

Pitch accuracy says nothing about segmentation. A detector can be perfectly in tune and still split one note into three. The note suite generates 60 melodies with 600 known notes and scores onset matching, pitch matching and F1 together.

04 Harmony

Key, voicing and the selector

Three different questions, three different tests: does the key inference find the key, does the voicing search move smoothly, and does the selector obey its own rules under random conditions?

05 The generated band

Held-out songs, three baselines

The MySong suite is the largest corpus test on the site: 44 songs the model never saw, scored at quarter-beat resolution against the lead sheet. The decoder, transitions and split are shared with the baselines, so the comparison isolates the model.

06 Voice and language

The parts you can hear, measured

Vocable pitch, vocable timbre, neural conversion and speech recognition each have a different failure mode, so each gets its own sample and its own chart.

07 Latency

What the app actually costs per request

Forty sequential requests per endpoint, after warm-up, on the same machine that serves the demo. Both the median and the 95th percentile are recorded, because a good median can hide a bad tail.

08 What a measurement changed

Six fixes that exist because a test failed

Pitch smoothing

Octave errors fell from 10.6% to 1.7%

The raw detector's p90 error was 1,182 cents, almost entirely octave jumps. The jump-confirm smoother requires two consecutive frames before accepting a large leap, bringing p90 to 16 cents without adding latency to real note changes.

Contour following

A frame-count window became a time window

The original lookback covered six frames, about 140 ms, which is shorter than a musical phrase. Fitting a slope over a 0.6 s window, in log frequency, is what made the mode track shape instead of jitter.

Drone

One note per phrase, not one per frame

Recomputing the drone from the latest note made it flip octaves mid-phrase and retrigger constantly. Locking it to the tonic, with hysteresis and a phrase-gap test, turned it into a held pedal.

Octave reinforcement

A low note needs a different vowel

The same vowel an octave down sounded muddy. A brighter, narrower vowel with a level trim that grows as the note descends is what makes the double read as a second singer.

Neural choir

Convert stems, not the mix

A mixed ensemble gives the voice model several pitches at once. Per-stem conversion keeps every call monophonic and lets harmony stems rotate across voices.

MySong data leak

A 0.73 result was withdrawn

Title-based deduplication left 40 of 44 test melodies in the training archive. A transposition-invariant signature check removed them, and the leaked score is not reported as a result. The clean number is 0.604.

The music

The voice comes first: a hand drum, a song, a word held across a note and then this system, answering without flattening it.

His songs work the way his performances do: a traditional object and a digital process set beside each other, each asking what the other is for. A hand drum carries a protocol. A sampler carries a protocol of its own. Almost all of the music treats that question as the material.

Cree vocables, the syllables a singer holds when the words step back, are the material this system works with. The singing stays Cree; the system follows it and stays out of its way.

This system is a new instrument for those songs: sing a line and it answers in a synthetic timbre, over the song's own instruments, on this machine and nowhere else. Nothing you record here leaves it.
