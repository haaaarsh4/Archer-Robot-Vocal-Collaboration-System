# Horse 

## Meaning

"Horse" is an imagined story set in 1868, shortly after the Medicine Lodge Treaty, in which a Cheyenne man and his family are living peacefully near the Washita River when an attack by General George Armstrong Custer's Seventh Cavalry begins. The narrator's horse suddenly speaks and, together with the other horses, turns against the soldiers, stopping the attack and preventing further violence.

The deeper meaning of the piece comes from the horse's final warning. The Horse People have protected and sacrificed themselves for humans, but they warn that humans will eventually abandon their relationship with animals for machines. The story connects the history of colonial violence with a larger warning about technology: humans may become so dependent on machines that they begin to treat machines as their relations and even change themselves to become more like them, while forgetting their relationship with animals, nature, and themselves.

## Backstory

"Horse" was originally written in **2001** for the Longest Night Festival in Whitehorse. Archer Pechawis describes it as an **historical re-imagining of the West**, rather than a straightforward retelling of documented history. The story takes place in the winter of 1868 and imagines an alternative version of the events surrounding Custer's Seventh Cavalry and the Cheyenne community at the Washita River.

The piece was originally performed as spoken word with a video backdrop. Over time, it developed into a larger multimedia performance combining **voice, drum, video, and cello**, with cello performed by **Cris Derksen**. In 2007, Pechawis developed a "digital drum", a hand drum containing a laptop that could play the video as part of the performance. This version premiered at the International Festival of Animated Objects in Calgary and was also performed at AKA Gallery in Saskatoon.

The story itself deliberately blends historical events with imagination and Indigenous perspectives. The narrator describes having lost family members at Sand Creek, marrying into Black Kettle's band, and eventually settling at the Washita River. When Custer's cavalry approaches, the horses become active participants rather than simply animals being used by humans. They recognize the violence that is about to happen and intervene to stop it.

The ending is especially important because the horse's warning moves the story beyond the historical setting of 1868. The horse predicts a future in which humans will replace horses and other animal relations with machines. In this sense, the historical story becomes a way of talking about the present and future, particularly about humanity's growing relationship with technology.

## Creation notes

The performance combines **spoken voice, hand drum, video, and cello**. The music and technology are not simply background elements. They help turn the story into a multimedia performance in which sound, image, rhythm, storytelling, and technology work together.

The title **"Horse"** is important because the horse is much more than a character in the story. The Horse People represent a relationship between humans and animals based on kinship, responsibility, loyalty, and mutual dependence. The horses are not treated as possessions or tools. They have their own knowledge, agency, and ability to make decisions.

The story also uses **"Garry Owen"**, an Irish marching tune that was historically associated with the U.S. Seventh Cavalry. In the story, the sound of the brass band playing Garry Owen signals the cavalry's attack. This creates a powerful contrast between the military music of the soldiers and the horses' own voices and actions. What initially signals military power eventually becomes part of a scene in which the horses themselves reject the soldiers' authority.

The spoken narrative moves from a quiet description of family life and peace into the chaos of the cavalry attack. The horse's ability to speak changes the meaning of the story. Instead of presenting the animals as passive participants in human history, the performance gives them a voice and allows them to act as protectors and teachers.

The final speech from the horse is the central message of the piece. The Horse People tell the humans that they will eventually forget what happened and abandon their relationship with animals in favour of machines. The warning becomes especially significant because the performance itself uses technology, including video and the later "digital drum", to tell the story. Technology is therefore both part of the artistic form and part of the question the work asks: **what happens when our tools become so important that they begin to replace our relationships with the living world?**

Ultimately, "Horse" is about **memory, colonial violence, Indigenous relationships with animals, technology, and the danger of forgetting our responsibilities to our relations**. The story asks us to consider what we lose when we stop seeing animals and the natural world as relations and begin seeing machines as replacements for those relationships.
